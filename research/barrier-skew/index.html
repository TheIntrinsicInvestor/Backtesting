Derivatives Pricing Study

The Barrier Premium: what the skew charges for a crash

A reverse convertible pays a high coupon because the investor sells a barrier put on the way in. I priced that put every week for thirty years, once with a flat volatility and once with a model fitted to the whole volatility skew, then checked both against what the market did. The skew charged 2.5 times the realised knock-in rate on the S&P 500, and 1.3 times on single stocks, which is where the notes are written.

01The Put Inside the Note

A barrier option is crash insurance sold by the buyer of a note

A reverse convertible looks like a bond. It pays a coupon far above the market rate and returns capital at maturity, unless the underlying falls through a barrier, commonly 30% below where it started. If the barrier breaks, the investor receives shares instead of cash and takes the loss. The coupon is the price of an option the investor sold on the way in: a down-and-in put, struck at the starting level, that pays out only if the barrier is touched.

Two things make that option harder to price than an ordinary put. The payoff depends on the path rather than only the final level, and the barrier sits far below the money, where implied volatility is much higher. On 3 May 2023 the S&P 500 traded at 4,091 with one year at-the-money volatility of 18.8%, while the volatility priced into options at the 70% barrier strike was 30.9%. Choosing one number to stand for that curve is the practical problem this report addresses.

No market print exists for a barrier option, because they trade over the counter. Every gap in the next two sections is therefore a gap between models rather than an error against a traded price. Section three is the exception: whether the barrier broke is a fact, and that is what the study tests.

02What the Skew Costs

Three models, three very different prices

Three models price the same contract. The first uses a single flat volatility taken at the money, the number a trader quotes when asked where the market is. The second uses a single flat volatility read off the skew at the barrier strike, which looks like the obvious correction. The third fits the whole surface and lets volatility vary with both price and time, the skew-consistent approach a derivatives desk would use.

Averaged over 1,507 weekly start dates on the S&P 500, a one year put with a 60% barrier was worth 3.54% of notional under local volatility, 0.80% under at-the-money flat volatility, and 5.43% under barrier-strike flat volatility. The naive at-the-money number is under a quarter of the skew-consistent one, and the obvious correction overshoots it by more than half.

Exactly one flat volatility reproduces the skew-consistent price, and it sits between the two candidates. At the 60% barrier it averaged 28.8%, against 19.5% at the money and 32.6% at the barrier strike. That spread is not constant: it widens when the skew steepens, so no fixed rule of thumb survives a change of regime.

03Implied Against Realised

The test: how often did the barrier actually break

This test has an answer outside the models. For every start date I recorded whether the underlying ever closed at or below the barrier during the following year. That is 1,507 products on the S&P 500 spanning 30 years, 1,044 on the Euro Stoxx 50 over 21 years, and 1,496 start dates for single stocks with ten large caps priced on each one, selected at the time by option volume so that no hindsight enters the sample.

At the 70% barrier the S&P 500 knocked in 6.4% of the time. Local volatility had priced 16.0%, at-the-money flat volatility 8.0%, and barrier-strike flat volatility 23.5%. Single stocks knocked in 26.7% of the time against a local volatility price of 35.7%. Every model overstated the danger. That is a risk premium: buyers of crash protection paid more than the crashes went on to cost.

One year products started every week overlap by fifty one weeks in fifty two, so the sample contains roughly 30 independent years rather than 1,507 independent observations. Intervals here come from a moving block bootstrap with one year blocks, and standard errors use a Newey-West correction at 52 lags. On that basis the S&P 500 gap at the 70% barrier (+9.6 points, 95% interval +2.6 to +15.1) excludes zero, as do the single stock gaps at 70% and 80%. The Euro Stoxx 50 carries the right sign at every barrier and reaches significance at none of them.

04Where the Premium Lives

The index is where the crash premium actually sits

The overcharging is uneven across underlyings. On the S&P 500, local volatility priced knock-ins at 2.5 times the realised rate. On single stocks the same model, fitted the same way, came in at 1.3 times. The Euro Stoxx 50 sits between them at 1.4 times.

Judged on cash profit alone, the two trades look identical. A seller of the single stock put collected 11.70% of notional and finished the year +3.45% ahead on average, against 4.93% collected and +3.38% earned on the index. What separates them is how much of the premium survives realised losses: the index seller kept 69%, the single stock seller 29%. The single stock premium is larger because single stocks are more volatile, and the seller pays almost all of it back.

That split is the correlation risk premium, measured in one contract. Driessen, Maenhout and Vilkov (2009) found that index options carry a large priced premium while individual equity options carry little, because the index premium compensates for correlation risk that single names do not bear. Their evidence is in variances. The same split appears here in the event that decides a note's payoff, whether the barrier breaks.

Sorting single stocks by their own volatility separates them further. The calmest third, averaging 24% volatility, kept 61% of the premium and behaves much like the index. The most volatile third, averaging 52%, kept 16%. Issuers do not write retail notes on the calm third.

05A Price, Not a Forecast

The skew misses the crash it is supposed to be pricing

If the skew were a forecast, its errors would scatter, and they do not. Grouped by era, the model is wrong in a pattern: it overstates knock-ins through every calm stretch and understates them in the one period that mattered. Products started during the financial crisis knocked in 40% of the time on the S&P 500 while local volatility had priced 25%. Through the 2010s the model priced 16% against a realised rate of 0%.

That pattern is what an insurance premium should do: exceed the average loss, then fall short in the disaster it insures against. It also settles what the skew is. The skew is a price, and anyone who reads implied probabilities off it as a forecast will be wrong in the period that matters most.

06The Note Buyer's Side

The premium is real, and the fee is bigger

Everything above measures the option, not the product. An investor does not receive the option premium. Issuers sell the note above its fair value, and the difference is their margin, which no term sheet states in a form a buyer can read off.

Published estimates of that margin are larger than the premium measured here. Vokata (2021) values more than 28,000 US yield enhancement products, the exact family studied in this report, and finds fees of 6-7% per year, with investors losing a comparable amount against risk-adjusted benchmarks. Henderson and Pearson (2011) put the overpricing of 64 issues at about 8% of issue price. The embedded put in our single stock sample earned +3.45% of notional a year before any costs. Subtract even the low end of those estimates and the arithmetic turns negative.

The comparison is indicative rather than exact. Our contract is a standalone one year put on one underlying, held to maturity, with no coupon and no call feature. The notes in those studies bundle a coupon, an early redemption trigger and sometimes several underlyings. What survives the difference is direction and order of magnitude: the risk premium inside the embedded option is similar in size to, or smaller than, the documented cost of reaching it through a note.

07Methodology

How the prices and the checks were built

Surfaces come from the OptionMetrics volatility surface file, which gives implied volatility on a delta grid at fixed maturities. For each date I fit an SSVI surface, a parameterisation that cannot contain butterfly or calendar arbitrage by construction, derive local volatility from it with Dupire's formula, and price the barrier on a finite-difference grid with daily monitoring.

I kept no result until three checks passed. The solver reprices plain puts to within one tenth of one percent of the Black-Scholes formula. Against Monte Carlo with daily monitoring it matches knock-in probabilities to within four hundredths of a percentage point. And because the barriers sit deeper than the grid the surface is fitted on, I tested the fitted wing against 762 individual listed put quotes between 55% and 75% of spot: mean absolute error 0.52 volatility points, and 0.69 points near the 60% barrier. The fit sits +0.41 points above the market on average in that region, which lifts local volatility knock-in probabilities slightly and therefore works against the central finding rather than for it.

I excluded surfaces that could not be fitted rather than flagging them: 620 of 14,960 single stock dates (4.1%), where fit error exceeded three volatility points or a tenth of at-the-money volatility. Most sit around special dividends and crisis weeks. One index date failed the same rule.

What could make this wrong

Overlapping windows Weekly start dates with one year products overlap almost completely. The effective sample is the number of independent years, not the number of products, and every interval quoted here respects that.
Few crashes Deep barriers break only in crashes, and the sample contains two or three. The 60% results should be read as descriptive.
One skew-consistent model Local volatility is not the only model that fits the surface. Stochastic volatility models match the same vanilla prices and can produce different barrier prices, because they assume a different future skew. That comparison is a separate study.
No hedging or funding Profit and loss is unhedged and held to maturity, and ignores funding, borrow and bid-ask spreads. A desk would hedge, which changes the distribution of outcomes rather than the expected value.
Three bugs found by the checks Placing the barrier on a grid node rather than between nodes inflated knock-in probabilities by about half a percentage point. The closed-form barrier formula overflows at very low volatility with large negative carry, which surfaced on Microsoft's 2004 special dividend dates. And mixing split-adjusted CRSP prices with unadjusted OptionMetrics spot turned every stock split into a false knock-in, briefly producing an impossible 40.8% realised rate before it was caught.
08Conclusions

What a structurer should take from this

Flat volatility misprices a barrier, and the choice of which flat volatility decides the direction of the error. At the money it understates the price by a factor of four at a 60% barrier. At the barrier strike it overstates it by half. The number that reproduces the skew-consistent price lies between them and moves with the regime, so there is no shortcut worth memorising.

Measured against thirty years of outcomes, the skew-consistent price was systematically generous to the seller of crash risk, by +9.6 percentage points of knock-in probability on the S&P 500 at a 70% barrier. That premium is concentrated in the index. On single stocks, and on volatile single stocks most of all, the bulk of what is charged for the barrier is paid back in realised losses.

For anyone structuring these products, an index-linked barrier and a single stock barrier are not two versions of the same trade. And for anyone buying the note rather than selling the option, the premium collected on their behalf is smaller than the published cost of the wrapper around it.
